## 8. "Walk me through your prospecting methodology"
**Likely rounds: R1, R2**

My prospecting approach is account-first, not contact-first. I start by building a tiered target account list — Tier 1 is my ICP sweet spot where I want maximum coverage across six to eight contacts per account, Tier 2 is opportunistic, and Tier 3 is watch-and-wait.

For Tier 1 accounts, I run a multi-channel sequence — email, LinkedIn, phone — but the outreach is always personalized to a specific business trigger. I'm looking for hiring signals, earnings call themes, news about digital transformation initiatives, or leadership changes. Those triggers give me a reason to reach out that's anchored in their world, not mine.

I layer in vertical-specific messaging. At Aisera I built outreach playbooks for BFSI and healthcare separately because the pain points, language, and regulatory contexts are completely different. That personalization significantly improved my response rates.

I also work referrals aggressively. Every closed deal becomes a potential introduction. And I leverage customer advisory boards and industry events strategically — not just to attend, but to get face time with accounts I'm targeting.

My average weekly prospecting cadence is five to seven meaningful touches per Tier 1 account on a rolling basis. I track everything in Salesforce and review my pipeline coverage every Monday — I want at least 3x coverage above quota at all times so I have room to work.

## 9. "How do you run discovery?"
**Likely rounds: R2, R3**

Discovery is the most important part of my sales process, and I run it like an investigation, not a questionnaire. My goal in the first discovery call is to understand three things: what is the business problem they're trying to solve, what happens if they don't solve it, and who in the organization owns that outcome.

I use open-ended questions and then I go deeper. If someone says "our planning process is too slow," I don't move on — I ask what slow actually means in terms of business impact. What decisions have been delayed? What did that cost? How does that compare to a peer company? I want them to tell me the story in their own words, not to confirm my assumptions.

I always map the stakeholder landscape in discovery — who's the economic buyer, who's the technical evaluator, who's the user champion, and who's the potential blocker. I try to understand their internal dynamics before I propose anything.

I also identify their implicit criteria early. The stated requirements are what they put in the RFP. The real criteria are what's driving the internal conversation. At Agiloft I've learned to listen for language around risk, compliance exposure, and board-level accountability — those are the actual drivers even when the stated need is "contract visibility."

By the end of discovery, I should be able to write their business case better than they can. If I can't, I haven't done enough discovery.

## 10. "How do you use MEDDPICC in your deals?"
**Likely rounds: R2, R3**

MEDDPICC is my deal operating system. It's not a checklist I fill out after the fact — it's the lens I use throughout the cycle to identify where I have gaps and what my next move needs to be.

The letters I spend the most time on are E, D, and C — Economic Buyer, Decision Criteria, and Champion. In my experience those are the three most common deal killers when they're poorly understood.

On Economic Buyer: I always push to get direct access, not just to coach through a champion. I'll ask my champion directly: "Who has final authority on this, and what would it take for you to feel comfortable introducing me?" If I can't get that meeting, I treat it as a deal risk and flag it in my forecast.

On Decision Criteria: I work with the champion to understand not just the stated criteria but the weighted criteria — what matters most internally when they're making the final call. Is it total cost? Implementation timeline? Vendor stability? The gap between what's in the RFP and what the committee actually debates is where deals are won and lost.

On Champion: I use a test I learned early in my career — a real champion is someone who will actively sell for you when you're not in the room. If they can't articulate the business case without me present, I haven't built a real champion. I invest heavily in enabling them.

I review my MEDDPICC hygiene on every deal in my weekly Clari review and use it to calibrate my forecast confidence.

## 13. "How do you handle pricing objections or procurement pressure?"
**Likely rounds: R2, R3**

Pricing objections are almost always a value gap problem, not an actual budget problem. When procurement comes back with "this is too expensive," the first thing I do is go back to the business case and figure out where the value didn't land.

My default response is to acknowledge and redirect: "I hear you on the budget concern — help me understand where the gap is. Is it a total cost issue, a timing issue, or is the business case not landing the way it needs to internally?" That question almost always opens a real conversation.

I've found that the deals where I hold price best are the ones where I've done the most rigorous business case work upfront. When the CFO can see a clear 3x or 5x ROI, procurement has less room to push on price because the economic buyer is already sold.

At Agiloft, I've had procurement teams push for 30% discounts on deals where I had full executive sponsorship. My approach is to offer flexibility on structure — payment terms, phased implementation, add-on services — rather than cutting license fees. That protects our margin and signals to the customer that the price reflects the value.

I've never given a discount I didn't have full champion alignment on first. If the champion can't defend the investment internally, dropping price won't fix the deal — it just accelerates a bad outcome.

I'll sometimes make a hard call to deprioritize a deal that has great conversation history but no clear path to a decision, and redirect that time to a deal I can actually close.

## 18. "What's your process for competitive deals?"
**Likely rounds: R2, R3**

Competitive deals start with intelligence, not assumptions. The first thing I do is find out which competitors are in the deal, what stage they're at, and — if possible — who they're talking to internally. I'd rather know I'm fighting Anaplan on day one than discover it in the final evaluation.

My approach is to compete on differentiation, not feature parity. If someone is comparing Pigment to Anaplan on implementation timelines, I want to anchor the conversation on speed-to-value and total cost of ownership — not on who has more formula options. I help the champion build evaluation criteria that play to our strengths, which is a legitimate part of the sales process.

At Agiloft I competed regularly against DocuSign CLM and Ironclad. My approach was to acknowledge what the competitors did well and then redirect to the dimensions where we clearly won — workflow flexibility, implementation speed, and total cost of ownership. Trying to bash competitors directly usually backfires with sophisticated enterprise buyers. They see through it.

I also take competitive losses seriously. Every deal I've lost to a competitor, I've done a debrief — what criteria drove their decision, what we could have done differently, what I'll bring to the next deal in that vertical. That institutional knowledge compounds.

For Pigment specifically, I've already started mapping the differentiation against Anaplan and Workday Adaptive. I know the core narratives: legacy architecture versus AI-native, implementation months versus implementation weeks, and configurability without professional services dependency. I'll go deep on those once I'm in the role.

## 23. "What do you think separates good AEs from great ones?"
**Likely rounds: R2, R3, Final**

Honestly, I think it comes down to intellectual honesty combined with genuine curiosity about the customer's business.

Good AEs run a solid process. They know the methodology, they hit their metrics, they close their quarters. Great AEs do all of that but they're also genuinely interested in the customer's problem — not as a means to close the deal, but because they want to understand it deeply enough to actually help. The other thing I'd say is self-awareness about where deals are actually at. The easiest trap in sales is optimism bias — believing your pipeline is stronger than it is because you're having good conversations. Great AEs are ruthlessly honest with themselves in their deal review. They know the difference between momentum and activity, and they're willing to make hard calls early — walk away from a bad deal, accelerate a good one, or call out a risk before their manager does.

I've also found that the AEs who sustain high performance over time are the ones who treat every loss as a learning event and every win as a system to replicate. They're not just good in the moment — they're getting better every quarter because they're deliberate about what they're taking from each cycle.

For me personally, the standard I hold myself to is: can my customer articulate the value they got from this deal independently of me? If they can, I've done my job.
